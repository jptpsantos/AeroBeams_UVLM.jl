           This paper analyzes the aeroelastic stability of a notional wing-propeller configuration
      exhibiting whirl flutter using the unsteady vortex-lattice method for the aerodynamics of both
      fixed and rotating lifting surfaces coupled with a linear beam model for the wing and Reed's two-
      degree-of-freedom model for the propeller. The influence of three-dimensional aerodynamics
      and aerodynamic interactions on the whirl flutter speed is systematically isolated and quantified
      for the first time. This is accomplished through output-based aeroelastic stability analyses
      using fully coupled or decoupled aerodynamic influence coefficient matrices and comparisons
      with eigenvalue-based analyses obtained using strip-theory aerodynamics for the wing and the
      Houbolt-Reed formulation for the propeller. The results are also compared with a published
      study employing a lifting-line approach combined with vortex-wake wing inflow and the viscous
      vortex-particle method propeller inflow. Aerodynamic interactions are shown to reduce the whirl
      flutter speed under both windmilling and thrusting conditions. However, they are partially offset
      by stabilizing three-dimensional aerodynamics, yielding a modest increase in the whirl flutter
      speed relative to predictions neglecting both effects. The results obtained using the unsteady
      vortex-lattice method closely agree with those from the published study, suggesting that wake
      modeling assumptions and viscosity effects do not significantly impact the whirl flutter speed.
      These findings elucidate the potentially competing roles of three-dimensional aerodynamics and
      aerodynamic interactions in the aeroelastic stability of wing-propeller systems and demonstrate
      the effectiveness of the unsteady vortex-lattice method for analyzing these configurations.

                                                 I. Introduction

    Growing interest in distributed electric propulsion [1-3] and advanced air mobility [4-7] has led to the development
of new aircraft concepts with multiple propellers on flexible wings. Many of these concepts combine vertical takeoff and
landing (VTOL) capability with energy-efficient forward flight, offering unique opportunities for localized transportation
of people and goods. However, they are susceptible to aeroelastic instabilities, with whirl flutter [8-10] being a primary
concern.

    Whirl flutter is a dynamic aeroelastic instability driven by mutual interactions among the inertial and gyroscopic
effects associated with a rotating system (a rotor or propeller), the elasticity of its support structure (a wing or pylon),
and the resulting aerodynamic loads [9]. Although Taylor and Browne first theorized the whirl flutter phenomenon in
1938 [8], it became a significant aircraft design concern only in the early 1960s, following two major flight accidents
caused by divergent whirl oscillations [11, 12]. These events highlighted the need for accurate aeroelastic stability
analysis of wing-propeller systems, a need that has reemerged with the rise of modern distributed electric propulsion
aircraft and electric VTOL concepts.

    Among the earliest investigations of whirl flutter, Bennet and Bland [13] derived an elastically mounted propeller
model for whirl flutter analysis and compared analytical predictions with experimental measurements. Two comprehensive
review articles [9, 10] subsequently surveyed the body of work on classical (propeller) whirl flutter and tiltrotor whirl
flutter until the early 2000s. More recent efforts have investigated the aeroelastic stability of wing-propeller configurations
using disparate modeling approaches, motivated by the push to develop new aircraft configurations with multiple
wing-mounted propellers. Hoover and Shen [14] analyzed NASA's X-57 Maxwell aircraft using a multi-body model
coupled with quasi-steady and unsteady lifting-line theory. Xu [15] analyzed a wing-propeller configuration by coupling
Reed's [9] rigid-blade propeller model with an Euler-Bernoulli beam-type wing model and strip-theory aerodynamics
based on Theodorsen's theory [16]. Böhnisch et al. [17] coupled Reed's propeller model to a wing aeroelastic model
composed of Euler-Bernoulli beam finite elements and the doublet-lattice method (DLM) [18]. Koch and Koert
introduced methods to couple linearized time-domain propeller models with DLM-based wing models [19], aiming to
enhance aerodynamic fidelity in frequency-domain flutter analysis of propeller-driven aircraft [20].

    The models employed in these studies neglected the mutual aerodynamic influence between the propeller and the
wing. Some recent efforts have examined these effects using mid-fidelity unsteady aerodynamic models. Bohnisch et al.
[21, 22] analyzed the aeroelastic stability of a wing-propeller configuration using the viscous vortex-particle method
(VVPM) [23] for the propeller aerodynamics and surface panels for the wing. They showed that aerodynamic interactions
reduce the wing flutter speed. However, their configuration did not experience whirl flutter. Chang et al. [24] examined
the effect of aerodynamic interactions on the whirl flutter speed of a wing-propeller system using transient simulations
from the Rotorcraft Comprehensive Analysis System (RCAS) [25-27]. Their study obtained the aerodynamic loads on
the wing and propeller blades using a lifting-line approach combined with either the uniform or vortex-wake (VW)

                                                                      2
inflow model for the wing wake and the uniform, dynamic, or VVPM inflow for the propeller wake. For the configuration
examined, aerodynamic interactions reduced the whirl flutter speed by up to 5% across both windmilling and thrusting
conditions. Simmons et al. [28] revisited the analysis by Chang et al. [24] to investigate amplitude-dependent effects
due to system nonlinearity using an output-based method [29]. Their study focused on previous RCAS simulations for
uniform wing inflow and VVPM propeller inflow. The results confirmed the destabilizing influence of aerodynamic
interactions on the configuration's whirl flutter mechanisms. In addition, they revealed nonlinear aerodynamic effects
at lower forward speeds, attributed to the propeller-wake effects on blade aerodynamic calculations. Kher et al. [30]
further analyzed the configuration of Chang et al. [24] for varying propeller spanwise placement and introduced a
wing-twin-propeller variant. Their study used RCAS simulations based on lifting-line aerodynamics, uniform inflow
for the wing, and the VVPM inflow for the propellers. They observed that a more outboard propeller placement
increased the whirl flutter speed and resulted in stronger destabilizing effects due to wing-propeller aerodynamic
interactions. Koch [31] investigated the role of aerodynamic interactions using a time-domain unsteady panel method
for both the wing and propeller. Harmonic forced-motion simulations were applied to identify frequency-dependent
generalized aerodynamic forces. The interaction terms were isolated by exciting one component while holding the
other fixed, identifying wing-to-propeller and propeller-to-wing interactions. The identified loads were them applied to
two simplified aeroelastic models: a two-degree-of-freedom wing model (heave and pitch) for classical wing flutter
and a two-degree-of-freedom isolated propeller-nacelle model. In contrast to previous studies [21, 22, 24, 30, 32],
aerodynamic interactions produced small stabilizing effects, although fully coupled wing-propeller flutter mechanisms
were not examined.

    These findings indicate that aerodynamic interactions between fixed and rotating lifting surfaces can degrade
aeroelastic stability of wing-propeller systems. However, several research questions remain open. First, most previous
work accounted for 3D aerodynamic effects only in the propeller wake (using the VVPM), but often neglected them
in aerodynamic load calculations on the wing and propeller blades (using lifting-line approaches) or even the wing
wake itself (using uniform inflow). Therefore, the relative importance and combined effect of 3D aerodynamics and
aerodynamic interactions remain poorly understood. Second, aside from an investigation into the influence of different
inflow models [24], no systematic comparison of results from distinct aerodynamic modeling approaches was conducted
for the same configuration. Therefore, it is unclear whether the observed aeroelastic stability trends can be captured by
modeling both the wing and propeller aerodynamics through a free-wake, potential-flow method.

    The overarching goal of this work is to advance understanding and prediction of wing-propeller aeroelastic
stability, specifically the influence of 3D aerodynamics and aerodynamic interactions alongside corresponding modeling
guidelines. The specific objectives of this study are: (i) to isolate and quantify the effects of 3D aerodynamics and
aerodynamic interactions on the whirl flutter speed of a notional wing-propeller system; and (ii) to determine whether
the influence of aerodynamic interactions captured using the UVLM aligns with previous findings from alternative

                                                                      3
aerodynamic modeling approaches. The test case is the wing-propeller configuration analyzed by Chang et al. [24]
and more recently revisited by Kher et al. [30]. This configuration is inspired by current electric VTOL aircraft and
was developed to exhibit whirl flutter as the critical instability mechanism [24]. A structural model of the system is
implemented by combining linear beam finite elements for the wing with Reed's two-degree-of-freedom model for the
propeller. This structural model is coupled with a free-wake unsteady vortex-lattice method (UVLM) [33] model for
both fixed and rotating lifting surfaces, and the resulting aeroelastic model is used to analyze aeroelastic stability via
transient simulations. The influence of 3D aerodynamics is first assessed by comparing whirl flutter speeds predicted by
assuming decoupled aerodynamic influence coefficient (AIC) matrices for fixed and rotating lifting surfaces against
those from aeroelastic eigenvalue analyses on a lower-fidelity model that employs unsteady strip theory for the wing
and the Houbolt-Reed quasi-steady formulation for the propeller. Next, the influence of aerodynamic interactions is
isolated from that of 3D aerodynamics by comparing results from two UVLM-based model variants with fully coupled
or decoupled AIC matrices. Lastly, comparisons with results from the published study of Chang et al. [24] highlight
the influence of different modeling strategies on the whirl flutter speed.

    The remainder of this paper outlines the theoretical formulation of the wing and propeller models (Sec. II) and
describes the test case used in the study (Sec. III. The results are then presented (Sec. IV), followed by concluding
remarks and an outlook on future research directions.

C. Unsteady Vortex Lattice Method
    The UVLM [33] is a 3D unsteady method used to calculate unsteady aerodynamic loads on lifting surfaces subject

to the incompressible flow of an inviscid fluid. This potential-flow approach discretizes each lifting surface and its
corresponding wake into panels and vortex-ring elements, assigning a collocation point to each panel and a circulation
to each ring. By applying a time-marching scheme, new wake panels and vortex rings are shed from the lifting-surface
trailing edge at each time step. For aeroelastic analyses, the spatial coordinates of these panels and vortex rings are

5
updated at each step to account for structural deflections. This work employs the UVLM as the aerodynamic modeling
approach for the entire wing-propeller system. The procedure for the aerodynamic analysis of propellers with the UVLM
is discussed in Refs. [43] and [44]. While linearized formulations of the UVLM in state-space form are available in
the literature [45, 46] and have been employed in fixed-wing aeroelastic stability analyses [47], this study uses a fully
nonlinear, time-domain implementation of the method.

boundary condition at each vortex-ring collocation point. This results in the following algebraic system:

The quantity AIC in Eq. (3) is the aerodynamic influence coefficient matrix, where each entry is derived from the
Biot-Savart law. The RHS term is the right-hand side vector, which accounts for the normal velocity components
due to rigid-body motion, structural deformation, gust perturbations, and the shed wake. Once  is determined by
solving Eq. (3), the wake is convected downstream with the local flow velocity. The aerodynamic loads, calculated
using Joukowski's theorem and the unsteady Bernoulli equation, are fed to the generalized force vector. The mapping
procedure used in this study assumes rigid chordwise cross-sections and coincident spanwise discretization for the beam
and UVLM models, following Ref. [48]. The aeroelastic equations are integrated in time using the Newmark- scheme
[49]. At each time step of a transient analysis, the circulations of the wing panels are solved based on the induced
velocities from the propeller and its wake, and the circulations of the propeller panels are simultaneously determined by
considering the wing's influence. Following the calculation of the bound circulations, the wake geometries for both
components are updated and convected downstream.

    The iterative time-stepping procedure described above captures wing-propeller aerodynamic interactions at each
temporal state [50] by calculating the induced velocity contributions from all surfaces and their respective wakes at
the collocation points of each vortex ring using the Biot-Savart law. For a wing-propeller system, the primary effects
of the propeller on the wing are an increase in local dynamic pressure and a change in the flow direction [51]. In
turn, the wing perturbs the propeller's inflow, altering the blade's effective angle of attack and aerodynamic loads.
Wing-propeller interactions can be eliminated from the aerodynamic analysis by setting the corresponding coupling
terms in the AIC matrix of Eq. (3) to zero. In addition, the RHS term in Eq. (3) must be assembled by ensuring that
the entry for a specific UVLM panel only accounts for the induced-velocity contributions from other panels on the
same lifting surface and its corresponding wake. This study follows these steps to isolate the effects of aerodynamic
interactions on the whirl flutter speed from those of 3D aerodynamics, while using a single aerodynamic modeling
approach for the whole system. Instead, previous studies in this area typically used different aerodynamic models for
the wing and propeller [21, 22, 24, 28, 30] without systematically isolating the effects of 3D aerodynamics from those

of aerodynamic interactions. In addition, in most of these studies, 3D aerodynamic effects were only included in the
propeller wake but neglected in the aerodynamic load calculations and wing wake [24, 28, 30].

    This study employs different methods to analyze aeroelastic stability depending on the aerodynamic model used.
When the aerodynamics are modeled using strip theory based on the formulation of Peters et al. [42] (wing) and the
Houbolt-Reed formulation [38] (propeller), the aeroelastic model can be cast in a state-space form suitable for direct
eigenvalue calculations [34]. Therefore, aeroelastic stability is analyzed by obtaining the system's eigenvalues while
sweeping through a parameter and by producing the associated frequency and damping diagrams. In this work, the
varying parameter is the forward speed, while all other parameters are fixed in a given aeroelastic stability analysis.
When the aerodynamics are modeled using the UVLM, aeroelastic stability is analyzed using an output-based approach
applied to transient simulations. First, the system's free responses to an impulse excitation applied on a selected DOF
are simulated across different forward speeds within a reduced range around the whirl flutter speed. This range is
estimated from the eigenvalue-based calculations. Next, moving-block analysis [52, 53] is applied to the simulated
time histories of a monitored variable to estimate the damping of the critical aeroelastic mode, corresponding to the
dominant frequency of each time history. Damping values are then interpolated to predict the whirl flutter speed.

    Moving-block analysis is selected for its ability to isolate specific frequency components in a time history [54]. This
approach to output-based damping estimation involves two major assumptions: linear (amplitude-independent) behavior
and small damping levels. The implications of these assumptions on whirl flutter prediction in nonlinear aeroelastic
systems are discussed in Ref. [55]. The validity of the linear assumption was verified by inspecting the natural logarithm
of the moving-block function, which exhibited the expected linear trend with time. Indeed, Simmons et al. [28] found
that free-wake nonlinearities, which are the primary source of nonlinear behavior in this study, do not significantly
impact damping levels near the whirl flutter point of the present wing-propeller configuration. The observed damping
levels are also small because simulations are only conducted relatively near the whirl flutter onset. Therefore, the
small-damping assumption is satisfied. Based on these remarks, it is concluded that the use of moving-block analysis for

                 7
output-based aeroelastic stability analysis is adequate for this work. Reference [29] discusses output-based approaches
suitable for wing-rotor and wing-propeller systems exhibiting stronger nonlinear behavior.

                                        III. Wing-Propeller Test Case

    Chang et al. [24] introduced the wing-propeller model analyzed in this study (see Fig. 1), inspired by current eVTOL
concepts [5]. The rectangular wing has a semispan of 7.5 m and a chord of 1.8 m. The structural model consists of beam
elements along the elastic axis, at 30% of the chord from the leading edge. The properties ensure that bending-torsion
wing flutter does not occur within the examined range of forward speed. The four-bladed propeller has a diameter of
2.3 m, a constant blade chord of 0.197 m, and linear twist varying from 62.6 deg at the blade root to 21.4 deg at the
tip. The propeller properties are scaled geometrically from an isolated, rigid-blade propeller used in previous work
[35, 36, 56]. The propeller rotates counterclockwise when seen from the front, and it connects to the wing's elastic
axis at a distance of 6.25 m from the wing root, corresponding to approximately 83% of the semispan. The mounting
stiffness is 18, 884 Nm/rad for both DOFs, considering a rigid-wing configuration. For the flexible-wing case, the
mounting stiffness is 19, 220 Nm/rad and 18, 916 Nm/rad for the pitch and yaw DOFs, respectively. These different
stiffness properties ensured similar absolute displacement of the propeller hub under the same load for the rigid- and
flexible-wing model variants [24]. The pivot length is 1.71 m.

    In the study of Chang et al. [24], the pylon was modeled using beam elements, with stiffness and inertial properties
selected to yield a whirl flutter speed below 100 m/s, consistent with the aerodynamic model employed. This study
accounts for pylon flexibility in a simplified way. Specifically, the pylon is modeled using a reduced representation
based on linear Euler-Bernoulli beam theory, with basis functions compatible with cantilevered boundary conditions
and the first bending deflection normalized by the pylon tip rotations. With this approach, the propeller pitch and
yaw DOFs are interpreted as generalized coordinates associated with the assumed out-of-plane and in-plane bending
displacements, respectively. This assumption primarily affects the inertial coupling between the wing and propeller
DOFs. The generalized inertia properties for the propeller DOFs are obtained from the reference natural frequencies
and stiffness [24]. This pylon model provides a simplified way to capture flexibility while preserving the reference
modal properties.

    Chang et al. [24] computed the aerodynamic loads on the wing and propeller based on the local effective angle
of attack using the lifting-line approach implemented into RCAS [25]. The wing aerodynamic model assumed a lift
curve slope of 2 rad-1, a drag coefficient of 0.01, and a quarter-chord pitching moment coefficient of zero. The wing
wake was modeled using a vortex-wake (VW) inflow model, and wing-to-propeller aerodynamic interactions were
captured using the simple horseshoe vortex interference model in RCAS [25]. The propeller blade aerodynamic model
assumed a NACA0012 airfoil lookup table with compressibility effects and used the VVPM [57] as the wake model.
Propeller-to-wing aerodynamic interactions were captured by accounting for the induced velocities from the VVPM

                                                                      8
particles on the wing when computing the local effective angle of attack for the lifting-line aerodynamic load calculations.
Even when aerodynamic interactions are not considered, RCAS simulations using the VVPM propeller inflow account
for particle-induced velocities on the blades when computing the propeller aerodynamic loads. References [24] provide
further details on the RCAS aerodynamic model.

    The wing structural model used in this work consists of 30 linear Euler-Bernoulli beam finite elements, with
properties from Chang et al. [24]. The UVLM aerodynamic model of the isolated propeller assumes 10 spanwise and
10 chordwise panels and uses an azimuthal step of 2.5. The aerodynamic model of the isolated wing consists of 30
spanwise and 10 chordwise panels. The wake of both the propeller and wing is truncated after 3 propeller rotations.
These parameter choices are based on convergence studies and are also used for the coupled wing-propeller model.

    The study of Chang et al. [24] considered both windmilling conditions, with a null propeller torque, and thrusting
conditions, with a target propeller thrust. The operational parameters for windmilling and thrusting conditions are
obtained from a periodic trim solution at a forward speed of 65 m/s and a wing-root angle of attack of 3 deg. This angle
of attack was selected to provide the required lift for an estimated dead gross weight of 17,800 N, representative of an
eVTOL aircraft. For the windmilling case, this condition produced an advance ratio of 1.33, corresponding to 1, 275
revolutions per minute (rpm). For the thrusting case, it produced an advance ratio and a propeller rotational speed of 1.26
and 1, 346 rpm, respectively, for the target thrust of 650 N. The advance ratios obtained from these trim analyses were
held constant with the forward speed in the aeroelastic stability analyses to maintain the same propeller aerodynamic
performance for comparison. This was accomplished by adjusting the propeller rotational speed to match the constant
advance ratio, and the same approach is followed in this study. Specifically, a trim analysis of the isolated propeller is
conducted at the same windmilling and thrusting conditions selected by Chang et al. [24]. The present model of the
wing-propeller configuration achieves the windmilling condition at a propeller rotational speed of 1, 208 rpm for an
advance ratio of 1.40, and the thrusting condition at 1, 313 rpm for an advance ratio of 1.29. These advance ratios based
on the present UVLM analysis are 5.26% and 2.38% higher for windmilling and thrusting conditions compared with
those by Chang et al. [24], respectively. These differences are attributed to the different wake treatments in the UVLM
and VVPM, the fully 3D aerodynamic load calculations considered in this work compared with the lifting-line approach
used in RCAS, and the NACA 0012 lookup table used for the propeller aerodynamic model in RCAS. However, the
obtained trim parameters are considered sufficiently close for the scope of this work.

4. Coupled Wing-Propeller System
    The wing-propeller structural model is first verified by comparing the wind-off natural frequencies in the undeformed

shape for a non-rotating propeller with the results reported by Chang et al. [24] for the flexible-wing variant. This
comparison is presented in Table 3. Without gyroscopic effects, the propeller pitch and yaw modes remain uncoupled.
Chang et al. [24] consider a flexible pylon model. The pitch and yaw modes defined in this study correspond to the
pylon's out-of-plane bending and in-plane bending, respectively. Chang et al. [24] also accounted for the pylon torsion,
introducing an additional mode not captured by the present model. The propeller lowers the wing's bending-dominated
frequencies compared to the isolated-wing case in Table 2 due to the additional inertia. The wing also affects the
propeller pitch and yaw frequencies. The pitch frequency increases compared with the isolated model due to coupling
with wing torsion, while the yaw frequency changes only slightly because the wing has higher in-plane bending stiffness.
Frequency differences compared with Chang et al. [24] remain below 3%, verifying the coupled structural model.

                                                 V. Conclusions

    This paper examined the roles of 3D aerodynamic effects and aerodynamic interactions in the aeroelastic stability of
a notional wing-propeller system exhibiting whirl flutter. The study used the UVLM for the aerodynamics of both fixed
and rotating lifting surfaces coupled with a structural model comprised of linear Euler-Bernoulli beam finite elements
for the wing and Reed's two-degree-of-freedom model for the propeller. The relative importance of 3D aerodynamic
effects and aerodynamic interactions was isolated and quantified for both windmilling and thrusting conditions through
systematic comparisons of output-based aeroelastic stability analysis results using fully coupled or decoupled AIC
matrices and eigenvalue-based results that neglected both 3D and interaction effects. Results were also compared

                                                                     21
against a published study for the same configuration modeled in RCAS, using a lifting-line aerodynamic load calculation
combined with a UI wing wake model and VVPM propeller wake model.

    The inclusion of 3D aerodynamic effects destabilized both the isolated propeller-pylon system and the complete
wing-propeller configuration, resulting in an increase in the whirl flutter speed of the order of 5%. This stabilizing
behavior was primarily attributed to the influence of the induced velocity from the propeller wake on the blade
aerodynamic load calculations. The 3D aerodynamic effects in these calculations were estimated to play a secondary
role, consistent with the blade aspect ratio of 6. Results from UVLM or VVPM wake modeling resulted in relatively
minor differences in the whirl flutter speed, indicating little sensitivity to the approach used to model the free propeller
wake. This finding was explained by the high downstream convection speed of the propeller wake near the whirl
flutter boundary. Wing-propeller aerodynamic interactions were found to destabilize the system, reducing the whirl
flutter speed of the complete configuration across both windmilling and thrusting conditions by the order of 2% to
3%. This finding aligns with previous studies based on lifting-line aerodynamics coupled with UI wing inflow and
VVPM propeller inflow. Hence, for the examined configuration, the combination of 3D aerodynamics and aerodynamic
interaction resulted in a partial cancellation of effects, leading to relatively small differences in the whirl flutter speed
compared with an eigenvalue-based aeroelastic stability analysis neglecting both physical mechanisms. This finding,
however, suggests that the differences may be significant for configurations where 3D aerodynamic effects play a
destabilizing role, as both these effects and aerodynamic interactions would contribute to decrease the whirl flutter
speed. Overall, the present analysis using the UVLM for both fixed and rotating lifting surfaces reproduced literature
trends, with relatively small numerical differences in the whirl flutter speed. These findings serve as cross-verification
of the previous work against an alternative mid-fidelity model and highlight the suitability of the UVLM for aeroelastic
stability analysis of wing-propeller systems.

    The conclusions from this study should be interpreted in the context of the characteristics of the wing model
considered to compare with literature results, specifically, its relatively high stiffness and natural frequencies. Increasing
wing flexibility is expected to both reduce the whirl flutter speed (and potentially introduce competing wing-dominated
flutter mechanisms) and shift the wing structural modes to a lower frequency range, which may cause higher-order
bending modes to participate in the critical instability mechanism. These effects are anticipated to increase the influence
of wing-propeller aerodynamic interactions due to the slower downstream convention of the propeller blade near the
flutter onset, and to introduce aerodynamically nonlinear behavior in the aeroelastic system [28]. In addition, the
participation of higher-order bending modes in the critical instability mechanism may change the character of 3D
aerodynamic effects [60, 62]. Therefore, 3D aerodynamics and aerodynamic interactions should be incorporated into
wing-propeller aeroelastic stability analysis to enable a systematic assessment of their effects.

    Future work will explore configurations with higher wing flexibility and multi-propeller arrangements. In addition,
it will incorporate geometrical structural nonlinearities to investigate post-critical conditions, as large-amplitude

                                                                     22
oscillations resulting from weak UVLM-induced nonlinear effects invalidate the linear beam structural model employed
in this work.
